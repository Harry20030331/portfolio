Reading [*Getting More*](https://www.penguinrandomhouse.com/books/201661/getting-more-by-stuart-diamond/) by Stuart Diamond inspired me to rethink how I approach problems at work and in my relationships. I’ve brought these ideas and my own reflections together into a framework I call **Constructive Thinking**.

> Understand people and their circumstances, turn the difficulty into a problem we can discuss, and find a next step together.

This framework preserves my own goals while making room for the other person's feelings, the facts, and feedback. It continues to evolve with experience.

## The Core Framework

| Question to ask myself | What I need to understand |
| --- | --- |
| **What am I trying to achieve?** | Am I trying to understand, help, negotiate, repair, or protect a boundary? Have I drifted into winning an argument or proving myself? |
| **How does the other person see it?** | What do they care about, worry about, and make of the situation? Ask and listen, then let them correct my understanding. |
| **Where are we actually stuck?** | Separate facts, assumptions, and judgments. Identify the specific needs, constraints, and disagreements. |
| **What can we try together?** | Compare options and tradeoffs, find small steps both sides can accept, and explain what I can take responsibility for. |
| **How will we check and adjust?** | Align expectations and responsibilities, use feedback to check progress, and decide whether to continue, pause, or leave. |

I do not need to ask every question in order. When emotions are strong, I can start by listening or pausing. When information is missing, learning a little more is already progress.

The goal of constructive thinking is to reformulate a difficult situation into a problem we can discuss and work toward solving together.

## Methods

I use the framework to identify where I’m stuck, then choose a method that helps me work through it. These methods evolve as I apply them and learn from experience.

### Understanding the Other Person

| Method | How I use it |
| --- | --- |
| **In their shoes** | Learn how they see the situation, reflect that understanding back, and give them a chance to correct it. |
| **Value people / Ask questions** | Show genuine interest in their circumstances, feelings, and concerns. Listen carefully and let their answers shape my response. |
| **Details** | Start with a concrete event and gradually clarify goals, reasoning, and obstacles. Check both sides' assumptions before dismissing something they care about. |
| **Emotional payment** | Respond with empathy, an apology, or a concession—the three forms discussed in my notes. The response should be sincere and appropriate, without empty politeness or a false admission of fault. |

Understanding others starts with sincerity: take a genuine interest in their experience and respond honestly. When expressing gratitude, explain what their help meant to you and the difference it made.

### Framing the Problem

**Notice my initial interpretation → Separate facts, assumptions, and goals → Understand both sides' circumstances → Restate the specific problem → Find an action to try.**

| Initial interpretation | A reframed question |
| --- | --- |
| I am too weak; I can only put up with it or get angry. | What do I need to stand firm on? How can I express it clearly, negotiate, or leave? |
| She is struggling, I cannot help, and the relationship feels beyond my control. | What do I still not understand? Which burdens could we help each other carry? |
| Doing badly means I am not capable. | Where exactly am I stuck? What information, experience, or resources are missing? |
| We have to settle who is right and who is wrong. | What does each person care about? Is there a shared goal or an arrangement we can discuss? |

Framing changes how I understand a problem, turning something that feels closed to discussion into a question we can examine and act on. It makes room for feelings and real disagreements; it does not guarantee that every problem can be solved. One outcome supports only a limited conclusion, not a verdict on a whole person, relationship, or life.

| Method | How I use it |
| --- | --- |
| **Personal judgment → Concrete constraints** | If the work takes thirty hours and only twenty are available, discuss time, scope, or support before concluding that someone lacks ability. Not every emotion reduces to a resource shortage. |
| **Complaint → Tradeoff** | Understand the need behind the complaint, then lay out options, benefits, and costs so we can discuss the tradeoffs together. |
| **Confrontation → Collaboration** | Find shared goals, facts, and standards. Keep the disagreement focused on the specific problem rather than a contest over who is better. |

### Finding Options and Support

| Method | How I use it |
| --- | --- |
| **Different preferences create value** | Find what each person values. Exchange something I can readily offer that matters to them for something they can readily offer that matters to me. |
| **Incremental steps** | Clarify one uncertainty, reduce one burden, or try one small step without requiring everything to be solved at once. |
| **Precedents and standards** | Look for cases with comparable conditions and clarify existing rules. Use verifiable evidence when discussing an arrangement. |
| **Support from others** | Find people who value my work and can actually help. Other parties may also clarify rules or coordinate disagreements. |
| **Internal justification** | Provide real contributions and evidence so a supporter can explain internally why the proposal deserves support. |

In workplace negotiations, I also check three things:

- **What does the other person gain?**
- **What does each of us give up?**
- **Am I meeting a real need and creating value that can be verified?**

### Checking Progress and Keeping Boundaries

| Method | How I use it |
| --- | --- |
| **Need → Evaluation → Match** | Clarify needs and evaluation criteria early, check gaps along the way, and adjust my actions. |
| **Trust but verify** | Give trust while agreeing on checkpoints. Use two-way communication to check progress and delivery and adjust when needed. |
| **Firm without aggression** | State my needs, reasons, and acceptable limits clearly. Apologizing for my tone does not mean withdrawing a reasonable request. |
| **Decide whether to continue** | When someone repeatedly puts me down or the conversation stops moving forward, I can pause, change the topic or approach, or leave. A boundary does not require their approval. |

## Three Reminders

- **Stay open and patient.** Give the other person room to express themselves, and let them correct my understanding.
- **Keep my goals and boundaries.** Take their perspective seriously while staying clear about my own needs and limits.
- **Turn care into action.** Feeling distressed does not mean I have done all I can to help. Care also means offering practical support and following through.
